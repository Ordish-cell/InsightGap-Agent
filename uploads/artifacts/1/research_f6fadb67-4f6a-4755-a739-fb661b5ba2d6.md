## Functional Equivalents Across Domains

Although the exact term lacks formal standing, several rigorously defined artifacts serve the *functional purpose* implied by the research brief: documenting, quantifying, and communicating meaningful differences in information—whether between claims and evidence, design intent and operational reality, benefits and harms, or model predictions and ground truth. These tools are standardized, mandated, peer-reviewed, or empirically validated within their respective domains.

### Health Regulatory Communication: FDA Drug Facts Box and EMA Benefit–Risk Summaries  
The FDA’s *Drug Facts Box* is a one-page, consumer-facing summary mandated for prescription drug labeling since 2006. It presents absolute numerical differences side-by-side: e.g., “11% greater clinically meaningful response vs. placebo” alongside “+21% incidence of akathisia” [4]. This format explicitly highlights *information differences*—quantified gaps between therapeutic benefit and adverse effect—using evidence from pivotal clinical trials. Its structure is codified in FDA guidance, its efficacy in improving risk comprehension has been empirically validated in randomized trials, and its content is legally binding [4]. Similarly, the EMA’s *Benefit–Risk Template*—developed through its Benefit-Risk Methodology Project—requires regulators to articulate divergent evidence streams (e.g., efficacy data from Phase III trials vs. safety signals from post-marketing surveillance), assign relative weight to each, and justify conclusions transparently [7]. Both tools institutionalize the comparison of discrepant information but operate under distinct nomenclature and governance—not as *“information difference cards.”*

### AI Transparency Frameworks: Model Cards and AI Cards  
In artificial intelligence, *Model Cards*—introduced by Google in 2018 and now widely adopted—include a dedicated *“performance metrics”* section that compares model output against ground-truth benchmarks across subgroups (e.g., accuracy disparity between age cohorts or demographic groups) [1][6][8]. This section inherently documents *information differences*: the gap between claimed capability and measured behavior. More recently, *AI Cards*—a machine-readable extension designed to align with the EU AI Act—formalize five core concepts, including *“Intended Use vs. Actual Use”* and *“Risk Profile,”* which explicitly encode discrepancies between design specifications and real-world deployment outcomes [10]. These artifacts are published alongside models on Hugging Face, referenced in EU Commission guidance, and cited in peer-reviewed journals like *npj Digital Medicine* [1]. Yet neither uses the phrase *“information difference card”*; their schemas define fields like *“evaluation_data”*, *“metrics”*, and *“limitations”*, not abstract “difference” containers.

### Climate Science Communication: IPCC Confidence and Likelihood Statements  
The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) employs a dual-metric framework to communicate scientific uncertainty and evidentiary divergence. *Confidence statements* (e.g., *very high*, *medium*) assess the degree of agreement and evidence robustness across studies; *likelihood statements* (e.g., *virtually certain*, *very likely*) assign probabilistic ranges to outcomes [11][12][13][15]. These are not standalone “cards” but embedded, calibrated linguistic conventions applied throughout AR6 reports—including the *Summary for Policymakers*—to signal where consensus ends and disagreement begins [12][13]. The AR5 Uncertainty Guidance Note (2010) mandates traceable justification for every judgment, ensuring that discrepancies among models, observations, and projections are made explicit and auditable—but again, no artifact bears the queried name [15].

### Environmental Risk Assessment: EPA IRIS Uncertainty Statements  
The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency’s Integrated Risk Information System (IRIS) assessments include formal *“Uncertainty and Discrepancy Statements”* that identify data gaps, methodological limitations, and conflicting evidence across toxicological studies [search reflection]. These statements are integral to IRIS’s regulatory decision-making process and undergo external peer review, yet they exist as narrative sections within longer technical documents—not as discrete, card-like artifacts [search reflection].
